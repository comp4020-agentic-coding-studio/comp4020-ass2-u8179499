# SLOP3550 — Buttons: Twelve Weeks of Pressing Things

Rules for building this course. They come from decisions already made and
approved; don't relitigate them while generating content.

## Course thesis

- The entire course studies digital buttons and the act of pressing them.
  Nothing else.
- Central question, always: "What makes a button feel worth pressing?"
- Do not widen the course into general UI, UX, HCI, web design,
  accessibility, ethics, psychology, or interaction design. Those words
  should rarely if ever appear as topic labels.
- A topic from that list may enter a week only as a property of one named
  button, never as its own unit. If a paragraph could be moved into a
  generic HCI course by deleting one sentence, rewrite it.
- Gestures are not substitutes for buttons. Every grounding example and
  activity must center on an actual bounded control that is pressed,
  clicked, or tapped — not a swipe, drag, pull, or other gesture — except
  when a week explicitly compares a button against a buttonless or
  gesture-based alternative.

## Curriculum coherence

- Twelve weeks, one progression, not twelve independent topics. The
  approved arc:
  1. The Button Before the Screen
  2. Translating the Push
  3. The Grammar of Pressable
  4. What the Button Promises
  5. Feedback and the Length of a Second
  6. The Button That Wants You Back
  7. The Button That Doesn't Work
  8. The Button You Stop Seeing
  9. One Button, Many Hands
  10. The Button With a Guard Rail
  11. The Button That's Lying to You
  12. The Perfect Button
- Each week asks a different question about the same object. Never
  collapse a week into a list of button properties (colour, size, hover,
  animation, sound).
- Later weeks cite earlier ones by name: Week 9 reuses the Week 7 crossing
  button, Week 10 reuses a Week 4 button, Week 11 names which Week 4
  promise or Week 6 reward loop it exploits, Week 12 synthesizes the
  whole semester into one final button. Preserve these callbacks; don't
  let a week stand alone if the approved arc gave it a predecessor.

## Concrete content

- Every conceptual claim is grounded in one specific, named real button —
  physical or digital. Reach first for doorbells, elevator buttons,
  pedestrian crossing buttons, panic buttons, delete buttons, purchase
  buttons, like buttons.
- No vague hypotheticals ("imagine a button that...") unless a real
  example genuinely cannot be found — and say so explicitly when that
  happens rather than silently inventing one.
- Every session ends with a concrete artifact, test, observation, or
  logged result — never a "reflect on this week's reading."

## Voice

- Short, declarative prose.
- Treat the premise with complete seriousness. Never explain or joke that
  studying buttons for twelve weeks is unusual. The humour comes entirely
  from the seriousness applied to a very small object — never from
  winking at the reader.
- No generic educational or AI prose: no "in today's digital world," no
  "students will explore," no "this week we will delve into."
- Avoid unless technically necessary: explore, delve, leverage, seamless,
  intuitive, unlock, journey.
- No fake enthusiasm, no unnecessary exclamation marks.

## Assessments

- Exactly three, forming analyse → build → defend. Do not add a fourth or
  rebalance the weights:
  - 20% — The Button Autopsy (analyse)
  - 35% — Build a Button Worth Pressing (build)
  - 45% — The Perfect Button (defend)
- Each assessment's brief names the specific weeks or concepts it draws
  on — don't leave the connection to the curriculum implicit.

## Deck

- Week 3, "The Grammar of Pressable," carries the one required real
  lecture deck.
- It teaches affordance, signifier, convention, and pressability through
  a fast sequence of concrete visual examples (real screenshots: things
  that look pressable and aren't, and the reverse) — not dense bullet
  slides restating the definitions in prose.

## Quality control

- Never accept malformed, truncated, placeholder, or corrupted prose.
  Reread generated text for broken words or cut-off fragments before
  accepting it.
- Don't copy the starter's placeholder structure mechanically — the
  placeholder shows the shape, not the content pattern to repeat.
- Don't give all twelve week pages an identical template of prose; the
  approved arc already varies what kind of week each one is (historical,
  experimental, analytical, ethical-by-way-of-one-button), and the pages
  should read that way.
- Every interactive idea must stay usable at both marking viewports
  (desktop and mobile).
