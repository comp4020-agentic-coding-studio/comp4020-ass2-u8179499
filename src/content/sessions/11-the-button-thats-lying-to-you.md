Three ideas from earlier weeks converge here. Week 4 said every button is
a promise about a state change. Week 6 said a button can be built to
reward repeated pressing regardless of whether the outcome matters. Week
10 said friction placed in front of a button can protect the person
pressing it. All three can also be pointed the other way: a promise can
be worded to obscure the state change, reward can be used to keep someone
pressing past the point it serves them, and friction can be placed
selectively — heavy on the choice you don't want taken, absent on the one
you do.

A button that does this isn't broken like Week 7's. It works exactly as
built. The manipulation is in which choice it makes easy and which it
makes costly, not in whether the mechanism functions.

## Find one, honestly

Find one real bounded button — not invented, not a vague composite —
where the wording, size, position, timing, visual weight, or surrounding
friction visibly pushes toward one option over another. Describe it
accurately enough that the leverage is checkable: what it says, where the
alternative option is, how it's styled, whether accepting is one press
and declining is several. If you can't find or verify a public example,
build and document your own instead of guessing at someone else's.

## Rebuild it honestly

Rebuild the same button, changing only properties of the button and its
immediate pressing interaction:

- wording
- size
- position
- timing
- visual weight
- confirmation or friction

Don't change what the button actually does. Only change what it's like
to press.

## Name the leverage

Write one paragraph naming exactly what the original version was using
against the person pressing it, and what specifically was lost — for that
person, not in the abstract — once you removed it. If nothing was lost,
say that too, and say what that tells you about whether the leverage was
doing any real work.
