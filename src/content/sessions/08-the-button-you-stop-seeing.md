By Week 6, repetition was interesting because the outcome varied. This
week repetition is interesting for the opposite reason: the outcome
doesn't vary at all, and that reliability is exactly what makes the
button disappear.

Press a light switch, a phone's volume button, or the send button in a
messaging app for the thousandth time and you will very likely not look
at it. You'll reach for its position, not its appearance. That's three
different things happening, not one:

## Three states, one button

- **Recognising the button** — knowing what it is and what it does, the
  first time you see it.
- **Attending to it** — consciously looking at it, checking its state,
  confirming you're pressing the right one.
- **Executing from habit** — pressing it correctly without attending to
  it at all, guided by its position rather than its appearance.

Week 3 was about signifiers — the visual cues that tell you a shape is
pressable. Habit is what happens after those cues stop being read. A
signifier only has work to do for as long as someone is still looking.

## One button, a thousand presses

Choose one digital button you press often enough that you no longer
consciously look at it before pressing. Over a defined period — a day, or
a fixed number of uses — log every press: the time, and whether you
looked at the screen to find it or pressed from memory.

## Two redesigns

Redesign the same button twice, without changing what it does:

1. For someone pressing it for the very first time — assume no memory of
   its position, and give it every signifier it needs to be found and
   trusted cold.
2. For someone who has pressed it hundreds of times — assume they will
   never consciously look at it, and design for the press, not the
   glance.

## Submit

Both redesigns, your press log, and one sentence naming what the two
versions assume the presser already knows. If the two redesigns look
almost identical, that's a result too — say what you tried to change and
why it didn't end up mattering.
