Week 4 placed five buttons on an axis from reversible to irreversible and
asked you to name what each one was actually claiming. This week takes
one of those five and asks what, if anything, stands between the press
and the consequence it claims.

Most consequential buttons have nothing standing between the two. A
delete button and a confirm-purchase button can sit at the same weight on
the axis and still differ completely in what it costs to press them by
accident.

## Five ways to add friction

- **Confirmation** — a second, separate press, asking whether you're
  sure.
- **Hold-to-confirm** — the press must be sustained for a fixed duration
  rather than instantaneous.
- **Delay** — the action is queued and only takes effect after a fixed
  interval, during which it can be reversed.
- **Re-entry** — the user must re-type or re-select something they
  already specified, rather than simply pressing again.
- **Typed confirmation** — the user must type a specific word, or the
  name of what they're removing, before the button becomes active.

Each of these costs the presser something — time, attention, an extra
step. None of them is free, which is exactly why the obstacle has to be
chosen for the specific button, not applied by default to every button on
the axis.

## One button, one obstacle

Take one button from your Week 4 table — pick one that sits toward the
irreversible end. Add exactly one obstacle from the list above, or one of
your own, before its consequence fires. Don't add two. Don't soften the
button itself; the obstacle sits between the press and the result, not on
the button.

## Justify the obstacle

Write, specifically for this button:

1. what the obstacle actually costs the presser, in time or attention
2. what it would take for the obstacle to fail to stop an accidental
   press — a case where someone gets through it without meaning to
3. why a weaker obstacle would have been insufficient for this button's
   actual consequence
4. why a stronger obstacle would have been unnecessary friction on a
   button people also need to press on purpose, often

Submit the button, the single obstacle you added, and the four answers.
An obstacle you can't justify against both failure directions is a guess,
not a guard rail.
