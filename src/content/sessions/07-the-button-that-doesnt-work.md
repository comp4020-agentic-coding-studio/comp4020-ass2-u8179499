A button that does nothing to the outcome it appears to control isn't
rare, though neither example below is a claim about buttons in general —
both are specific, documented cases.

New York City's Department of Transportation has stated publicly, and
outlets including the New York Times have reported, that most of the
city's roughly 3,250 pedestrian crossing buttons are mechanically
disconnected from the signal timing — left on the pole after the lights
were automated, because removing thousands of buttons cost more than
leaving them. This is documented for New York specifically; it is not a
claim about crossing buttons everywhere, and many installations elsewhere
do control the signal.

Elevator door-close buttons are a separate, similarly reported case —
covered by outlets including the New Yorker and IEEE Spectrum: in many
U.S. buildings the button does nothing, because building codes require
doors to stay open long enough for boarding regardless of the button, so
a working button would undercut that requirement. This isn't true of
every elevator; some, particularly outside U.S. building-code
requirements, do have a functioning door-close button.

The point isn't that buttons are secretly fake. It's that a button can
retain every signifier Week 3 named — the raised icon, the click, the
light that comes on when you press it — while making a promise, in Week
4's terms, that nothing on the other end can keep.

## What a working press can't teach you

A button that works confirms the wrong thing: that pressing it and the
outcome happening are connected. A button that doesn't work is the only
way to find out how long you'll trust that connection anyway, and what
you do once you stop.

## A crossing button that doesn't cross

Build a digital pedestrian crossing button. On press, give it full
acknowledgement — a light change, a sound, a vibration, whatever your
platform can do — exactly as a real one would. Underneath, wire it to
nothing: whatever "signal timing" your version simulates should run on
its own schedule, completely unaffected by the press.

## The suspicion log

Have someone use it without telling them what it does. Let them press it
as many times as they want, across as long a session as you can arrange.
Log the press number, or the elapsed time, at which they first do
something that shows they suspect the button isn't working — pressing
repeatedly and fast, giving up, pressing something else instead, or
asking you directly.

Note, in one sentence, which real signifier your version borrowed from an
actual crossing button — the exact detail that made it read as functional
before anyone doubted it.

## Keep this button

Don't discard it after logging the result. Week 9 comes back to this
exact crossing button.
